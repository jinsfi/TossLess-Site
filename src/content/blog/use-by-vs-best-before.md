The date on a food label is mostly a quality date, not a safety date. The US Department of Agriculture (USDA) says that, apart from infant formula, dates are not an indicator of safety. If a date passes while you have the food at home, USDA says it should still be safe and wholesome if it was handled properly, until you can see or smell that it has spoiled.

## Quick answer

| Label | What USDA says it means | Is it a safety date? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best if Used By / Before | When the product will have its best flavor or quality | No |
| Sell-By | How long the store should display the product for sale | No |
| Use-By | The last date recommended for using the product at peak quality | No, except on infant formula |
| Freeze-By | When to freeze the product to keep its peak quality | No |

You may also see "best-before" on labels. USDA's own phrase is "Best if Used By", and it recommends that manufacturers and stores use that one, because research shows it makes the meaning clearer.

## Why the dates are on the food

The manufacturer or store picks the date to show when the food will be at its best. It takes into account how long the food is in transport and on the shelf, the temperature it is kept at, the recipe and the packaging. Because of these factors, the dates on similar foods can differ.

There is no single wording that all labels must use in the United States, so you will see many different phrases. Federal law does not require dates on food, with one exception: infant formula.

## When a date does matter

Infant formula is the exception. USDA says formula must carry a "Use-By" date, because using it by then ensures it still contains each nutrient in the amount listed on the label. USDA's advice is not to buy or use baby formula after that date.

For everything else, the date is not what keeps you safe. How you stored and handled the food is.

## How to tell it's gone bad

USDA says spoiled food develops an off odor, flavor or texture. If it has, don't eat it. Check the food before you eat it, whatever the date says.

One warning from USDA: a change in the color of meat or poultry is not a sign of spoilage.

## How to make it last longer

Dates assume the food was handled well. USDA's example: food left out above 40°F (4.4°C) for more than two hours, or more than one hour when it is 90°F (32.2°C) or hotter, should not be eaten. USDA also says to refrigerate eggs in their original carton, in the coldest part of the fridge and not in the door.

## Common questions

### Can I eat food after the best-by date?

USDA says food that shows no signs of spoilage should be wholesome and may be eaten, sold or donated after the "Best if Used By" date. The quality of perishable food may get worse after the date, so check it first.

### Does "use-by" mean I must throw it away on that day?

Not for food safety. For most foods it is the last date recommended for the best quality. The exception is infant formula, which you should not use after its date.

### Do canned goods have a date?

Cans must carry a code or the canning date. USDA says these codes are not meant for you to read as a "Best if Used By" date, and that some cans also show an open date. USDA says high-acid canned foods, such as tomatoes and fruit, keep their best quality for 12 to 18 months, and low-acid canned foods, such as meat and vegetables, for two to five years. Throw away cans that are dented, rusted or swollen.
